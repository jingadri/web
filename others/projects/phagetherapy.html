Co-evolution of Bacteriophage within Bacterial Microbiomes

How the PhD moves from isolating Klebsiella pneumoniae phages to testing therapy, resistance, community change and plasmid transfer in a clinically relevant model of polymicrobial catheter-associated urinary tract infection.

1 · Project overview and research question

The project in one view
Central question: how do phages and bacterial hosts co-evolve inside a multispecies infection, and can that knowledge produce effective phage therapies without destabilising the microbiome or accelerating the spread of antimicrobial-resistance plasmids?

The project focuses on phages that infect K. pneumoniae, an opportunistic pathogen that can form extensive catheter biofilms and shelter other pathogens. Instead of studying phage–host interactions only in liquid monoculture, it uses a polymicrobial in-vitro CAUTI model containing both free-living and catheter-associated biofilm populations.

Therapeutic killing, evolutionary response and gene-transfer risk are connected outcomes—not separate afterthoughts.

Objective 1 · Phage discovery

Isolate phages against clinical K. pneumoniae, define genomes, capsids, host range and biofilm activity, then examine resistance and phage combinations.

Objective 2 · Therapy in a community

Compare individual phages and combinations against planktonic and biofilm populations in a polymicrobial CAUTI model, before and after co-evolution.

Objective 3 · Plasmid transfer

Test whether phage exposure changes movement of plasmids carrying phage-defence systems, antibiotic-resistance genes, or both.

Do not reduce the PhD to “find a phage that kills bacteria.” The project asks whether killing remains effective in a community, how resistance reshapes both partners, and whether therapy changes the mobility of resistance genes.

2 · Domain knowledge and key terms

Why context changes phage therapy

Phage infection

A virulent phage adsorbs to a susceptible bacterium, injects its genome, replicates and lyses the cell. Productive infection depends on receptors, intracellular compatibility and bacterial defence.

Biofilm infection

Catheter biofilms contain cells embedded in an extracellular matrix. Spatial structure, growth state and neighbouring species can alter access, physiology and treatment susceptibility.

Polymicrobial ecology

Several bacterial species share resources and modify the environment. Removing one member can release competitors, change biofilm structure or indirectly protect susceptible cells.

What happens when a lytic phage meets a bacterium

Important: this is the general lytic sequence. The exact receptor, capsule-degrading activity, defence system and lysis machinery must be established for each phage–host pair.

Host-cell and microbiome state: without versus with phage

DNA-transfer question: plasmid conjugation is shown separately from phage infection. The project tests whether phage killing and selection change plasmid transfer or enrichment; it should not assume that the therapeutic phage itself transfers the plasmid.

CAUTI has two connected habitats

HabitatWhat is measuredWhy it matters
Planktonic populationCells and phage in bladder-like liquid; density, composition and titres over time.Shows dispersal and killing in a mixed, well-sampled phase.
Catheter biofilmAttached biomass, viable counts, spatial structure and species composition.Represents the protected infection reservoir and may respond differently from liquid.
Whole modelFlow, catheter colonisation, blockage-related phenotypes and exchange between phases.Reveals effects that static monoculture assays cannot reproduce.

Three evolutionary processes to keep separate

  • Phage resistance: bacterial changes reduce adsorption or block infection; these changes may carry fitness costs.
  • Phage counter-adaptation: variants expand or recover infectivity against resistant hosts.
  • Horizontal gene transfer: plasmids move between bacteria, potentially spreading antibiotic resistance and phage-defence systems independently of mutation.
Key distinction: a broad host range across strains is not the same as a broad host range across species, and neither guarantees performance inside a biofilm community.

3 · Research idea, hypotheses and novelty

From pairwise killing to community evolution
HypothesisTestResult that challenges it
Phages selected against diverse clinical isolates can provide useful breadth.Host-range matrices, efficiency of plating, adsorption and killing curves across isolates.Activity is narrow, unstable, or lost in representative urine/biofilm conditions.
Combinations can increase coverage and delay resistance.Compare single phages and rational cocktails through replicated serial passage.The mixture shows antagonism, rapid cross-resistance or no benefit over its best component.
Community membership changes treatment efficacy and co-evolution.Run matched monoculture and polymicrobial CAUTI models; sample both planktonic and biofilm fractions.Effects are indistinguishable after controlling for density, habitat and sampling.
Phage-driven loss of K. pneumoniae reshapes the wider microbiome.Measure absolute and relative abundances of all members through time.No reproducible community response, or apparent change is a compositional artefact.
Phage exposure changes plasmid transfer or selection.Track donors, recipients, transconjugants and plasmids with and without phage.Transfer rates and plasmid persistence remain unchanged across justified conditions.
Novel combination: the project integrates directed evolution, genomics, metagenomics and biochemical phenotyping within a tractable clinical microbiome model. It connects phage efficacy to host evolution, community dynamics and mobile resistance rather than optimising killing in isolation.

These are testable interpretations of the advertised objectives. Exact organisms in the community, phage numbers, passage regimes and assay endpoints remain design choices for the student and supervisory team.

Isolate and purify K. pneumoniae phages

Lab · discovery
InputEnvironmental samples and bacterial hosts.
ActionEnrich, plaque-purify repeatedly, amplify and quantify independent isolates.
Output / gateClonal, reproducibly lytic phage stocks with secure host and provenance records.
Risk / returnMixed isolates or temperate behaviour → repurify, reclassify or exclude.

Characterise genome, capsid and infection biology

Lab + compute · identity
InputPurified phages and high-quality DNA.
ActionSequence and annotate genomes; assess morphology/capsid; measure adsorption, latent period and burst behaviour.
Output / gateDistinct, well-supported phage identities without obvious lysogeny, toxin or AMR liabilities.
Risk / returnIncomplete assemblies or unsafe cargo → resequence or remove candidate.
4

Map host range and biofilm activity

Lab · phenotype
InputPhage collection and strain panel.
ActionBuild quantitative host-range matrices and test killing of established and developing biofilms.
Output / gatePhages with complementary, reproducible activity under relevant conditions.
Risk / returnSpot clearing without productive infection → confirm by plaque formation and replication.
5

Resolve resistance mechanisms and fitness costs

Evolution + genomics
InputIndependent resistant clones and matched ancestors.
ActionPhenotype cross-resistance; sequence clones/populations; validate candidate mechanisms by targeted genetics.
Output / gateMechanistic link between genotype, resistance and fitness or virulence trade-off.
Risk / returnOne clone or one endpoint overstates repeatability → use independent lines and time series.
6

Co-evolve phage and host

Directed evolution
InputDefined phage–host pairs, controls and passage design.
ActionSerially passage replicated populations; archive timepoints; measure reciprocal infectivity through time.
Output / gateReproducible evolutionary trajectories with frozen historical samples.
Risk / returnPopulation collapse or contamination → recover archived points and adjust selection regime.
7

Design and challenge phage combinations

Lab + human · therapy design
InputHost-range, receptor, resistance and interaction data.
ActionCombine complementary phages; test coverage, synergy/antagonism and resistance delay against singles.
Output / gateCombination outperforms justified single-phage comparators without hidden interference.
Risk / returnCross-resistance or antagonism → redesign components using mechanism, not count alone.
8

Test therapy in the polymicrobial CAUTI model

Lab · clinical model
InputSelected singles/cocktails, validated community and catheter model.
ActionTreat matched models; sample planktonic and biofilm fractions at multiple times and doses.
Output / gateReduced target burden and biofilm with interpretable effects on every community member.
Risk / returnLiquid-phase success but biofilm failure → revisit delivery, timing and matrix access.
9

Track populations, genomes and community response

Lab + compute · ecology
InputPre/post-treatment samples and archived time series.
ActionCombine culture counts, phage titres, isolate genomics and metagenomic profiling.
Output / gateAbsolute and relative changes agree sufficiently to support ecological conclusions.
Risk / returnRelative abundance alone creates false “growth” → add absolute quantification.
10

Measure plasmid transfer under phage exposure

Lab + genomics · biosafety
InputMarked donors/recipients, defined plasmids and phage treatments.
ActionQuantify donors, recipients and transconjugants; confirm plasmid identity and defence/AMR phenotypes.
Output / gateTransfer frequency or rate is normalised to viable populations and genetically verified.
Risk / returnSelection changes cell survival rather than transfer → measure all denominators and use no-phage controls.
11

Integrate efficacy, evolution and risk

Human + statistics · decision
InputKilling, resistance, community, transfer and reproducibility data.
ActionCompare predeclared endpoints and trade-offs; identify robust treatment rules and unresolved risks.
Output / gateA therapy claim bounded by model, strains, timing and safety evidence.
Risk / returnOptimising one endpoint hides harm elsewhere → reject single-metric ranking.

5 · Validation plan and success measures

What each claim needs
ClaimMinimum evidenceEssential controlsSuccess measure
Phage infects a strainPlaques plus productive replication; quantitative efficiency relative to propagation host.Uninfected bacteria, phage-only sterility and non-host strain.Reproducible infection, not spot lysis alone.
Cocktail improves therapyMatched single-phage and mixture dose–response across strains and replicates.Equal total phage dose, vehicle and untreated infection.Greater breadth, killing or resistance delay than the best justified single.
Phage disrupts biofilmViable target counts plus biomass/structure; planktonic fraction measured separately.Untreated biofilm, matrix-only/sterility and time-zero baseline.Target reduction in attached population without artefactual redistribution.
Community changesSpecies-resolved absolute counts supported by sequencing composition.No-phage time series and monoculture comparator where informative.Repeatable change beyond normal community drift.
Co-evolution occurredReciprocal time-shift infection assays plus genomic change in independent lines.Unpassaged ancestors and bacteria-only/phage-only passage controls.Heritable, repeatable change in infectivity or resistance.
Phage affects plasmid transferGenetically confirmed transconjugants normalised to donor/recipient populations.No-phage, no-donor, no-recipient and plasmid-free controls.Consistent treatment effect separated from differential killing.
Do not infer transfer from resistance phenotype alone. Confirm that putative transconjugants contain the expected plasmid and distinguish increased conjugation from simple enrichment of pre-existing plasmid carriers.

Experimental design essentials

  • Use independent biological replicate communities and evolutionary lines, not repeated measurements from one model.
  • Randomise treatments and predefine primary endpoints, sampling times and exclusion rules.
  • Archive ancestral and intermediate phage/bacterial populations so mechanisms can be revisited.
  • Report limits of detection and handle zero counts without silently substituting arbitrary values.
  • Analyse planktonic and biofilm compartments separately before combining conclusions.

6 · Evidence, risks, limits and failure paths

What is established—and what remains open

Established platform

The supervisory team has published catheter models in which phage treatment delayed or prevented Proteus mirabilis catheter blockage, and a reproducible polymicrobial CAUTI model that supports both planktonic and biofilm communities.

New research space

Those precedents do not establish efficacy against K. pneumoniae in the advertised community, predict co-evolutionary outcomes, or determine how treatment affects plasmid mobility.

RiskWhy it mattersDiagnostic resultReturn path
Rapid resistanceInitial killing may be transient.Target rebounds with heritable resistance.Map mechanism; redesign cocktail or exploit fitness trade-offs.
Community protectionNeighbours or matrix can shield the target.Monoculture killing but poor community/biofilm activity.Change timing, dose, delivery or phage selection.
Ecological releaseRemoving Klebsiella may expand another pathogen.Target falls while a harmful member rises absolutely.Broaden endpoints or redesign targeted combination.
Plasmid-mediated defenceMobile defence can spread phage resistance.Acquisition of plasmid co-segregates with reduced infection.Validate causality and select phages that bypass defence.
AMR mobilisationTherapy could enrich or spread resistance determinants.Verified increase in AMR-plasmid transfer or persistence.Change treatment design; define contraindication and mechanism.
Model dependenceIn-vitro urine flow is not a patient immune system.Effect changes with medium, strain, catheter or timing.State scope and validate across justified model conditions.
Boundary of the evidence: success in the CAUTI model supports a mechanistic and preclinical claim, not clinical efficacy. Pharmacology, host immunity, manufacturing and regulation lie beyond what this in-vitro project alone can establish.

8 · Applicant preparation

Fit, skill gaps and interview prompts

Useful starting strengths

  • Microbiology, aseptic technique and quantitative culture.
  • Interest in bacteriophage, AMR, biofilms or microbial ecology.
  • Experimental design and careful work with time-series models.
  • Willingness to combine wet-lab data with genomics and bioinformatics.

Skills the project can develop

  • Phage isolation, purification and infection assays.
  • Dynamic polymicrobial CAUTI and biofilm models.
  • Directed evolution and reciprocal infectivity testing.
  • Genome/metagenome analysis and plasmid tracking.
  • Integration of therapeutic, ecological and evolutionary evidence.
Interview questions to rehearse
  1. Why might a phage work in planktonic monoculture but fail in a polymicrobial catheter biofilm?
  2. How would you distinguish resistance evolution from transient physiological tolerance?
  3. What makes a rational phage cocktail better than simply combining the broadest phages?
  4. How would you show that phage exposure changed plasmid transfer rather than only changing donor or recipient survival?
  5. Why are absolute population counts needed alongside metagenomic relative abundance?
  6. What result would make an apparently effective treatment unsafe or unsuitable?

9 · Glossary, papers and project sources

Compact reference
Essential terms
Bacteriophage (phage) — a virus that infects bacteria.
Biofilm — surface-associated microbial community embedded in extracellular material.
CAUTI — catheter-associated urinary tract infection.
Directed evolution — repeated selection and propagation used to enrich variants with a phenotype.
Efficiency of plating — plaque production on a test host relative to a reference host.
Host range — bacterial strains on which a phage can productively infect.
Horizontal gene transfer — movement of genetic material between organisms rather than parent to offspring.
Plasmid — independently replicating DNA element that can carry AMR or phage-defence genes.
Polymicrobial — containing multiple microbial species.
Transconjugant — recipient bacterium that acquired a plasmid by conjugation.
Time-shift assay — tests hosts and phages against partners sampled from past, present and future timepoints.
Virulent phage — phage whose productive cycle culminates in host lysis, contrasted with temperate integration.
