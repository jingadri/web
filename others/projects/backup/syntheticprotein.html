Part 2

Biology

What the pathogen does, and what the plant has to work with

Essential plant immunity

Why the synthetic receptor exists

PTI: external detection

Cell-surface PRRs (pattern-recognition receptors) detect PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns) — bacterial flagellin, fungal chitin. Co-receptors and kinases then fire Ca²⁺ influx, ROS (reactive oxygen species), MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase) cascades and defence genes. This layer is PTI, pattern-triggered immunity.

Effectors: pathogen interference

Bacteria, fungi and oomycetes deliver effectors that alter plant processes; some suppress PRRs, immune kinases, signalling or downstream responses. Not every effector is an immunity suppressor.

ETI: internal detection

ETI, effector-triggered immunity: intracellular NLRs detect effectors directly, or detect their effects on host proteins. Activation can lead to resistosome assembly, immune signalling and sometimes hypersensitive cell death.

PTI in detail: who touches whom

A ligand binds its PRR, a co-receptor joins, and a receptor-like kinase fires three outputs at once. The dashed arrow is the chitin branch converging on the same machinery. This is the layer effectors are sent to dismantle.

Why a second layer exists: suppression and counter-detection

Surface detection works, so pathogens evolved effectors to shut it down from inside; plants then evolved intracellular receptors that detect those effectors. Your synthetic receptor adds to that third layer — it does not replace PTI.

How effectors get inside

Two delivery routes, both in scope
No “receptor X” opens the T3SS. That idea comes from animal pathogens — in Salmonella and Yersinia, host-cell contact does trigger secretion. In plant pathogens none has been established. The T3SS switches on before contact, from apoplast chemistry: pH ~5.5, low osmolarity, nitrogen and carbon starvation, plant sugars and phenolic acids. The bacterium reads “I am in a leaf”, not “I am touching a receptor”. A plasma-membrane sensor aiding translocon assembly is proposed, not established.
Infection does not switch on your receptor. It is a transgene, already expressed and waiting in the cytoplasm — it has to be, or detection comes too late. Infection triggers everything downstream; the plant’s response is the output, not the on-switch.

Both columns matter. Half 1 spans bacterial, fungal, oomycete and other pathogens because a SUSS family can cross them; narrowing early throws away the breadth the strategy depends on. Delivery differs completely, but the design problem — a folded effector surface in the cytoplasm — is identical.

What both routes share, and why it matters for design: whichever way an effector arrives, the form your binder has to recognise is the mature, folded protein sitting in the plant cytoplasm — not the form it travelled in. Bacteria make this vivid: a T3SS effector is threaded through a ~2–3 nm channel unfolded and only refolds once inside, so the surface you are designing against does not exist during transit. Fungal effectors are secreted folded, but still end up in the same place. The design consequence is the same for both: when you build the conformational ensemble in The one face that is never hidden, the states that count are the intracellular ones, including the effector bound to its plant target.

No pathogen builds an injection machine in soil. One cue switches on machine and cargo together, because every gene in the set shares one promoter signature.

Layer 2 · Order and timing decide which one first

So “which effector” is partly a transcription question and partly a queueing one. The wave that matters for your receptor is 2 — those are the cytoplasmic effectors, delivered while the plant is still defensible.

Layer 3 · For fungi, the stage of infection decides

Fungi have no such cascade. Effector genes are tied to developmental stage, and the sets barely overlap:

Destination splits too: apoplastic effectors stay outside and are cysteine-rich; cytoplasmic ones go through the BIC and carry more positive charge. That compositional difference is what effector-prediction software exploits (Finding real effectors).

Why two strains of the same pathogen carry different effectors

Effector repertoires are under constant selection, and this is the single most important fact for your design choices:

  • Recognition is a liability. An effector the plant can detect is an avirulence factor — it gets the pathogen killed. Strains under pressure from a matching resistance gene mutate that effector or delete it outright.
  • Redundancy makes deletion cheap. Several effectors often target the same plant pathway, so dropping one costs little fitness. This is why targeting a single dispensable effector fails in the field.
  • Host range follows the repertoire. Which plants a strain can infect is largely determined by which effectors it carries.

Where NLRs are, and reading calcium

Localisation, channels and the early read-out

Where NLRs are, and what activation actually does

Inactive NLRs are mostly cytosolic; some are nuclear or membrane-associated. Target and receptor must share a compartment.

Why calcium is measured

Resting cytosolic Ca²⁺ is low, so an influx acts as an early second messenger. Its amplitude, onset, duration and oscillations form a calcium signature. GCaMP (a genetically encoded fluorescent Ca²⁺ indicator) reports it as fluorescence; aequorin as luminescence.

Recognition, signalling and protection are separate experimental milestones. Cell death alone does not establish authentic or useful immunity.

Is killing your own cell really the immune response?

Plants have none of the machinery that question assumes: no mobile immune cells, no antibodies, nothing that engulfs a pathogen, and nothing that travels to the infection site. Every cell defends itself with what it already contains.

What the intuition expects

Detect the invader, send cells to it, destroy and clear it, remove the toxins it released. That is roughly how vertebrate immunity works.

None of it is available here. In particular, there is no way to remove an effector once delivered. The plant cannot un-inject it or neutralise it.

What the plant actually does

It removes the resource instead of the pathogen. The infected cell and its immediate neighbours die deliberately and fast — the hypersensitive response (HR).

Most are biotrophs or hemibiotrophs: they need living host cells. Killing the cell destroys the niche, so the pathogen is starved and walled into dead tissue — containment by denial.

HR is the most visible output, not the most important. Alongside it the cell induces defence genes, makes antimicrobial compounds, reinforces its wall with callose and raises salicylic acid to prime the rest of the plant. Death alone skips the useful part.

So why is cell death not accepted as proof? Three reasons, and each has sunk real projects:
  • It is not specific. Plant cells die from overexpression, mis-folded protein, toxicity and ordinary stress. Seeing death tells you something happened, not that immune signalling happened.
  • It helps the wrong pathogens. Necrotrophs feed on dead tissue. Against them an HR is actively counterproductive.
  • Constant death is a dead plant. An autoactive receptor kills tissue with no pathogen present. That is a stunted or dead crop, which is why the OFF state is weighted so heavily throughout this reference.

Hence the “binding ≠ activation ≠ resistance” principle in Strategy at a glance, and why calcium and defence-gene induction are measured rather than dead leaf patches. The target is a receptor that stays silent, then produces a real defence programme — with localised death as a consequence, not the goal.

Receptor architecture

Sensor, helper, binder — and what we replace

Pikm-1 sensor + Pik-2 helper

Schematic, not a scale model or established mechanical explanation of activation. CC, coiled-coil; HMA, heavy metal–associated integrated domain; NB-ARC, nucleotide-binding domain; LRR, leucine-rich repeats.

Read the diagram above as a reference design, not as the plan. Pik plays three roles here, and they are easy to run together:

1 · A worked example

The one NLR whose integrated-domain slot is known to accept foreign recognition modules — other HMAs, nanobodies, AI-designed binders. Study it to learn how such a receptor is put together.

2 · A control and a benchmark

Early candidates can be tested in it: working positive control, published structures, known-compatible helper, obtainable plasmids. Anything new has to beat it.

3 · Not what you deliver

Swapping the HMA for a designed binder is the published precedent, and it mostly failed — 2 of 46 (Validate before the lab). The advert asks for the next step: receptor architectures designed from scratch. Succeeding only at a Pik graft reproduces published work.

Sensor, helper, binder — three different things

The analogy: the binder is the sensor head you unscrew and replace; the sensor NLR is the alarm unit; the helper NLR is the siren. Swapping the head changes what triggers the alarm, not the alarm or the siren.

Sensor and helper must be a matched pair. A good binder can still give an unusable receptor if they are not: Pikm-1OsHIPP43 was autoactive with Pikm-2, well behaved with Pikp-2 — same binder, same sensor, different helper (Engineering ON and OFF states).

Is the HMA the original bait, and are we replacing it?

Yes — and that is what the published work did. A minority of NLRs carry an integrated domain: a copy of a host protein fused in as bait. Pikm-1’s is the HMA, copied from the host proteins AVR-Pik effectors evolved to attack. Replacing it with a designed binder is the move that slot was shown to tolerate — the thing to learn from, not the architecture to hand in.

Avoid the word “universal”. The binder covers a structural family, not everything. Say family-level or broad-spectrum — accurate, and it does not invite the obvious challenge.

What “new-to-nature architecture” means — and what it does not

Not Pikm-1 with a fluorescent protein bolted on: a tag is an instrument, the architecture is the receptor. New-to-nature means a designed sequence found in no organism, keeping the domain organisation — recognition module, nucleotide-binding switch, regulatory region — but engineered to be soluble, expressible, purifiable and tractable for structure work.

Part 3

Strategy — the full flow

Start to finish: pick a target, design a binder, prove it works

Strategy at a glance

Keep this visible while designing

Not “pick one effector and make a binder” — the goal is shared structural features across entire effector families.

Non-negotiable principle: binding ≠ immune activation ≠ disease resistance. A receptor is useful only if it is quiet without the target, activates specifically when the target is present, and produces meaningful defence.

OFF state

No cognate effector: intact, sufficiently expressed receptor with minimal spontaneous immune signalling. Avoid constant cell death or growth penalties.

ON state

Cognate effector present: target binding is coupled to receptor activation, calcium signalling and defence gene induction.

Proof of value

Receptor works against relevant effector variants and eventually reduces infection. Strong affinity alone is never a pass.

Recommended order of decisions

Decisions 3–5, unpacked

The three that sound simple in the diagram above, and hide most of the real work.

3 · Choosing a scaffold

“Validated” means someone has already published a working integration into it. Five checks:

  • An integrated domain you can replace, rather than an insertion point you must invent?
  • An experimental structure, ideally with an effector bound?
  • A documented compatible helper?
  • Works in Nicotiana benthamiana, the standard test plant?
  • Plasmids obtainable — host lab, collaborator or repository?

Pikm-1/Pik-2 passes all five. A practical starting choice, not a scientific preference.

4 · Why several binders

Not conformations of one binder. Several independent designs — different backbones, different sequences — all aimed at the same surface.

The scaffold stays constant and only the binder module changes, so any difference in expression, OFF-state or signalling is attributable to the binder.

Diversify twice: backbones from RFdiffusion, then sequences per backbone. Per-candidate success is low and unpredictable, so the panel is the unit of work.

5 · Sites, junctions, helpers

Three different things, easily run together:

  • Integration site — where the binder goes. In Pikm-1 that is the HMA (heavy metal-associated) domain, sitting between the CC and the NB-ARC (nucleotide-binding) domain.
  • Junction — the residues joining binder to receptor. Too rigid and it cannot fold or reach; too floppy and binding is not transmitted.
  • Helper — the partner NLR that signals (Pik-2). The choice measurably changes autoactivity: same binder, same sensor, different helper can flip a receptor from autoactive to well-behaved (Engineering ON and OFF states).

Finding real effectors

What to actually type, and where names come from
Start here: searching UniProt for the word effector does not work. There is no UniProt keyword for "effector" — the closest is Virulence (KW-0843). Most real effectors are annotated only as "uncharacterised protein", having no recognisable domains: they are defined by what they do, which databases cannot infer from sequence. So never "search for effectors" — look up the handful already known, or predict new ones from a whole proteome.

Scaling this across many pathogens

The funnel processes one proteome; the project needs tens. Pick a handful per pathogen and pool them? No — that destroys the thing you are looking for.

Why pre-selecting breaks it. A SUSS family is invisible until you cluster by structure — the project's entire premise. Hand-pick five per pathogen and you cannot tell one family's five members from five unrelated singletons: blind sampling from the dimension you are measuring. Worse than neutral: every available criterion (“best characterised”, “most cited”, “already has a structure”) biases toward well-studied effectors — the ones natural resistance genes already recognise and pathogens have learned to shed. Predict everything; let the clustering define the families.

The discovery set — exhaustive

Every effector candidate from every chosen pathogen, deduplicated at high identity, structures predicted, clustered all-against-all.

Its job: reveal families you did not know existed, including any spanning pathogen kingdoms.

The control set — small and curated

A few dozen experimentally confirmed effectors with known structures: AVR-Pik, Pwl2, AvrPto, Avr3a and similar.

Run it through the pipeline and ask: does my clustering recover the known MAX family? RxLR-WY? This validates the method; it discovers nothing.

A hand-picked set checks the pipeline.

The one face that is never hidden

Picking a surface that survives every fold
Three separate problems, often confused

"Target a conserved surface" sounds simple until you notice it has to survive three different kinds of variation at once:

ProblemWhat variesWhy a naive target fails
1 · Family diversityDifferent effectors, same fold, as little as 15–20% sequence identityA binder keyed to specific side chains binds one member and ignores its relatives
2 · FlexibilityLoops, tails and termini of a single effector move constantlyA patch that looks exposed in one AlphaFold model can be covered in the next
3 · State changesFree vs bound to its plant target; monomer vs oligomer; before vs after processingYour binder may be designed against a surface that is occupied in the cell

A single predicted structure hides all three problems, because it shows you one member in one state. That is the trap.

The idea in one sentence: instead of asking "what does this effector look like?", you ask "which patch of surface is still exposed in every member of the family and in every shape each one adopts?" — and you aim only at that. Because that patch is present whatever you meet, a binder against it cannot be dodged by folding differently or by swapping in a relative.

Finding it: a filter that only subtracts

Why step 4 is the whole method: a residue buried half the time still looks fine on an average. Only the minimum exposure across the ensemble answers “never hidden”. Steps 5–8 only ever remove residues — nothing is added back.

"Challenging" the effectors: the validation matrix

Finding a candidate face is a hypothesis. You then attack it deliberately, and this is where most candidates die:

Cross-conformer testre-dock the binder against every conformer in the ensemble — not just the one you designed on
↓
Cross-member testpredict against every family member; this measures the breadth you are claiming
↓
Escape testmutate each epitope residue in silico. If one point mutation kills binding, the epitope is too fragile — reject it
↓
Specificity testscreen against the plant proteome. Any cross-reaction is an autoimmunity risk, which kills the plant line

Only a binder that passes the whole matrix is worth making. The scoring is ruthless deliberately: you keep what works against everything, not what scores best against your favourite model.

Why this buys durability

Which effector, when explained that pathogens escape recognition by mutating or deleting the detected effector. The invariant face is chosen specifically to close both exits:

Escape routeWhy it is blocked
Mutate the recognised surfaceThe patch is functionally constrained — changing it costs virulence, so escape has a fitness price rather than being free
Delete the effectorYou chose a core, non-redundant effector, so losing it is expensive
Swap in a different family memberThe binder was validated against the whole family, so relatives are recognised too
Fold or flex differentlyThe face is exposed in every conformer in the ensemble, by construction
The honest limits. The ensemble is only as good as its sampling — AlphaFold seeds explore far less than real conformational space, so "exposed in all conformers" means "in all the conformers you generated". The functional-constraint step depends on knowing what the effector does, and for most effectors nobody knows. And an invariant face can still be a poor binding site: flat, featureless, or too polar to grip. Expect to lose most candidate epitopes here, and treat the lab as the real arbiter — which is what Wet-lab validation and What establishes functionality are about.

Wet-lab and structural validation

Where crystallography belongs

The wet-lab order is gates 6 to 11 in the stage gates — build and verify constructs, confirm the protein is made, establish association, test ON/OFF in planta, then commit structural resources. What follows is what each structural method actually answers.

Crystallography: what it answers

Whether the binder contacts the predicted surface, which residues interact, and whether side-chain geometry matches the model. It can reveal unexpected orientations, and is usually applied to a purified binder–target complex rather than the whole NLR.

Cryo-EM: what it answers

For sufficiently stable complexes, it can reveal oligomeric architecture and large conformational rearrangements in inactive versus active receptor states. Capturing both states is difficult and may itself be a major PhD objective.

Verified structural precedent: the 2024 Pwl2–OsHIPP43 complex was determined by X-ray crystallography (PDB 8R7A), illuminating a robust effector-binding interface. It did not by itself solve the complete Pik receptor's ON/OFF mechanism.

Co-IP: how it works and how to read the blot

Co-immunoprecipitation asks one question: if I grab protein A out of a plant extract, does protein B come with it? The two proteins carry different tags so they can be grabbed and detected independently.

1 · Co-expressBait (binder or receptor, GFP-tagged) and prey (effector, HA-tagged) together in N. benthamiana leaf.
2 · ExtractGrind tissue in lysis buffer. Keep a small portion as the input — proof both proteins were made.
3 · CaptureAdd beads carrying anti-GFP antibody. They hold the bait, and anything bound to it.
4 · WashRinse away everything not held. Wash strength is the main dial between losing real partners and keeping junk.
5 · Elute & blotRun SDS-PAGE, transfer, probe once with anti-HA and once with anti-GFP.

What a clean positive result looks like

Each tag is detected separately. The anti-GFP row confirms the bait was captured; the anti-HA row reports whether the effector travelled with it.

Putting it together. A functional fusion must pass several at once: quiet alone (1), fit slot (2) and helper (3), bind (4), relay (5), tolerable dose (6). What this does not show. Requirements were not measured per binder, are not independent, weights unknown. Lesson, itself an inference: design for requirements 1–3 and 5, not only binding — keep and re-surface the natural HMA scaffold, match size and termini, test helper alleles.

4Rules to raise success and cut waste

  1. Plan for low single-digit success. 2 of 46 (FoSSP17), 0 of 44 (AVR-PikF): about 2% pooled, wide interval. Budget dozens of designs per target; at 2–4% that is roughly even odds of one hit, not a guarantee (my arithmetic, not from a source).
  2. Do not assume binding metrics predict signalling. Binding did not predict outcome (tightest, Fobinder5, repressed; Fobinder9 bound, no response). Per-binder ipTM/ipAE are unpublished for FoSSP17, so untestable here. KD about 1–15 µM is a plausibility bound from the two functional binders (1.29 and 13.9 µM), not a validated gate.
  3. Screen the helper first. Pikm-2, Pikp-2 and at least one more Asp230 helper (Pikps-2 or Pike-2) with every fusion, before any structural effort.
  4. Fusion geometry gate. Model the full chimera; binder termini at roughly the HMA termini spacing, keep the HMA flanking residues, reject clashes with CC/NB-ARC.
  5. Epitope gate. Design against the target’s biological state: FoSSP17 is a dimer and 8 of 12 characterised binders sit on the face its dimer interface occludes. Prefer free-face epitopes, but expect that to fix binding and SEC behaviour, not signalling.
  6. Low-dose, controlled assays. Precedent: OD600 0.5 NLR and effector, 0.1 p19, 4-week-old plants, 5 dpi, 0–6 score, 3 biological replicates (Myc:FoSSP17 and AVR-PikD lowered to 0.1 for westerns); controls empty vector, AVR-PikD, Pwl2. Add wild-type Pikm-1 + AVR-PikD, a non-binding mutant, an empty-ID fusion, a western for expression — so autoactivity is not read as success.
  7. Treat charge as a weak hypothesis. Track net charge and hydrophobicity of every tested binder, to test it beyond 12 (part 2 above).
  8. Log every outcome against every score (the stage gates), so filters improve each round.

7What cannot be validated on a computer

  • Whether a fusion is autoactive: no validated predictor. Public cell-death scores exist for Pik-1/Pik-2 allele pairs and HMA variants (Table S4 of Xi 2026, Bentham 2023), none for de novo binder fusions beyond the 12 named FoSSP17 binders.
  • Effector-triggered signalling, Ca²⁺ flux and resistance in planta.
  • Expression dose, and the right helper for each fusion.

All 12 characterised Fobinders associated with FoSSP17 in co-IP (selected from 46), yet only 2 of 46 signalled correctly — binding is not limiting here, but that is one target, not a general rule.

Papers and what they teach

Six sources worth reading properly

Xi et al. (2026), bioRxiv preprint ↗

Directly relevant to Bentham's PhD. RFdiffusion + ProteinMPNN designed integrated domains for Pikm-1 against FoSSP17 from Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense TR4. Binding and structural accuracy did not uniformly predict signal relay. Preprint: subject to revision.

Zdrzałek et al. (2024), engineered OsHIPP43 receptor ↗

Natural effector-binding HMA domain replaces Pikm-1 HMA; Pikm-2 pairing autoactivated, whereas Pikp-2 allowed Pwl2-dependent response. Solved binder–effector crystal structure (PDB 8R7A). Practical precedent for binding, helper choice and structural validation.

Bentham et al. (2023), allelic compatibility ↗

Pik helper alleles regulate autoactivity; residue 230 is influential in tested combinations. Of four autoactive nanobody integrations, three were alleviated by Pikp-2, but one remained autoactive and another showed weaker signalling. Do not generalize helper rescue.

Kourelis et al. (2023), Pikobodies ↗

Nanobody modules can replace natural integrated recognition domains to produce ligand-dependent synthetic plant NLRs. Engineered constructs detected fluorescent targets and protected plants from engineered viruses.

Zhu et al. (2026), programmable SPIRs ↗

Programmable designed modules targeted viral, bacterial, fungal and oomycete proteins. In-planta directed evolution improved activity and reduced autoactivation; transgenic plants showed resistance. Demonstrates design-and-selection, not guaranteed first-pass success.

Durham PhD advert (2026) ↗

Two research strands: map structurally related effectors including SUSS families; design new-to-nature, tractable receptor architectures with RFdiffusion/ProteinMPNN/BindCraft, X-ray/cryo-EM and authentic calcium/gene-expression validation.
