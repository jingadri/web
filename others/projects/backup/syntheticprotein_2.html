Synthetic Plant Immune Receptors

How to design a target-binding module, integrate it into an NLR (nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptor), and distinguish a functioning immune switch from a binder that is dead, autoactive or nonspecific.

Tailored to Durham's “Designing Immunity from First Principles” PhD. Demonstrated Pik-1/Pik-2 systems are a reference platform; the advertised project extends beyond them toward new-to-nature receptor architectures. Project advert ↗

01. The strategy at a glance

Keep this visible while designing
Non-negotiable principle: binding ≠ immune activation ≠ disease resistance. A receptor is useful only if it is quiet without the target, activates specifically when the target is present, and produces meaningful defence.

OFF state

No cognate effector: intact, sufficiently expressed receptor with minimal spontaneous immune signalling. Avoid constant cell death or growth penalties.

ON state

Cognate effector present: target binding is coupled to receptor activation, calcium signalling and defence gene induction.

Proof of value

Receptor works against relevant effector variants and eventually reduces infection. Strong affinity alone is never a pass.

Recommended order of decisions

  1. Choose a relevant, accessible pathogen effector or conserved effector fold.
  2. Examine conserved, exposed sites across natural variants.
  3. Choose a validated NLR starting scaffold and suitable controls.
  4. Design and rank several binder candidates, not one.
  5. Compare integration sites, junctions and known-compatible helpers.
  6. Reject unstable, inaccessible and autoactive candidates early.
  7. Independently establish expression, binding and signalling.
  8. Structurally characterize promising functional constructs.
  9. Test recognition breadth and actual pathogen resistance.

Decisions 3–5, unpacked

The three steps that sound simple in the list above but hide most of the real work.

3 · Choosing a scaffold

“Validated” means someone has already published a working integration into it. You are not assessing NLRs in the abstract — you are looking for one with a track record. Test it against five questions:

  1. Does it carry an integrated domain you can replace, rather than needing you to invent an insertion point?
  2. Is there an experimental structure of it, ideally with an effector bound?
  3. Is a compatible helper documented?
  4. Does it function in Nicotiana benthamiana, the standard transient-expression test plant?
  5. Are the plasmids obtainable — from the host lab, a collaborator or a repository?

For this project Pikm-1/Pik-2 is the answer to all five, which is why it is the reference platform. That is a practical starting choice, not a scientific preference.

4 · Why several binders

Not different conformations of one binder. Several independent designs: different backbones, different sequences, each a separate protein aimed at the same surface on the same target.

You are right about the second half, and it is the important part: the receptor scaffold stays constant and only the binder module changes. Because everything else is held fixed, any difference in expression, OFF-state behaviour or signalling is attributable to the binder.

Diversify on two axes: several backbones from RFdiffusion, then several ProteinMPNN sequences per backbone. Design success per individual candidate is low and not predictable in advance, so the panel is the unit of work, never a single design.

5 · Sites, junctions, helpers

Three different things that are easy to run together:

  • Integration site — where in the receptor the binder goes. In Pikm-1 the natural HMA sits between CC and NB-ARC; that position is the validated one.
  • Junction — the handful of residues joining binder to receptor on each side. Too short or rigid and the binder cannot fold or reach; too long or floppy and binding may not be transmitted to the switch.
  • Helper — the partner NLR that performs signalling once the sensor detects (Pik-2). “Known-compatible” means a published pairing; the choice measurably changes autoactivity.

Evidence boundary: RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN and AlphaFold can help design and screen structures. They cannot establish that an NLR will switch ON, nor predict calcium signatures or plant disease resistance reliably. Those require experiments.

02. Essential plant immunity

Why the synthetic receptor exists

PTI: external detection

Cell-surface PRRs (pattern-recognition receptors) detect conserved microbial molecules — PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns) — such as bacterial flagellin or fungal chitin. Co-receptors and kinases then trigger Ca²⁺ influx, ROS (reactive oxygen species), MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase) cascades and defence genes. The whole layer is PTI, pattern-triggered immunity.

Effectors: pathogen interference

Bacteria, fungi and oomycetes deliver effectors that alter plant processes; some suppress PRRs, immune kinases, signalling or downstream responses. Not every effector is an immunity suppressor.

ETI: internal detection

ETI, effector-triggered immunity: intracellular NLRs detect effectors directly, or detect their effects on host proteins. Activation can lead to resistosome assembly, immune signalling and sometimes hypersensitive cell death.

PTI in detail: who touches whom

Two example branches. A ligand binds its PRR, a co-receptor is recruited, and a receptor-like kinase passes the signal to three outputs at once: calcium, ROS and a kinase cascade. The dashed arrow marks the chitin branch converging on the same machinery. This is the layer effectors are sent in to dismantle.

Why a second layer exists: suppression and counter-detection

The sequence that makes the whole field make sense: surface detection works, so pathogens evolved effectors to shut it down from inside; plants then evolved intracellular receptors that detect those effectors. Your synthetic receptor is an engineered addition to that third layer — it does not replace PTI, it adds a new detection event on top of it.

Where NLRs are

Many inactive NLRs are cytosolic. Others are nuclear or membrane-associated, depending on receptor type. Some activated CNLs (coiled-coil NLRs, named for their CC N-terminal domain), notably ZAR1, assemble membrane-associated Ca²⁺-permeable resistosomes; do not assume all NLRs, or Pik-1/Pik-2 specifically, operate as identical calcium channels. Target and receptor must occupy compatible cellular compartments.

What that warning does and does not mean. It does not mean “all NLRs are calcium channels, just built from different parts”. It means something stronger: most NLRs have never been shown to be channels at all. One receptor, ZAR1, was demonstrated to form a membrane pore, and that single result became the mental picture for the whole family. It should not be. Why this matters for your experiments, not just your vocabulary. Cytosolic Ca²⁺ can rise for many reasons: a receptor acting as a pore, downstream channels opening, or general stress. So when your GCaMP or aequorin trace goes up, you have evidence that immune signalling occurred. You do not have evidence that your receptor is a calcium channel. Those are two different claims needing two different kinds of experiment, and conflating them is one of the easiest mistakes to make when writing this work up.

Why calcium is measured

Low resting cytosolic Ca²⁺ allows Ca²⁺ influx or release to act as an early second-messenger signal. Its amplitude, onset, duration and oscillations form a calcium signature, decoded by Ca²⁺-responsive proteins into downstream responses. GCaMP (a genetically encoded fluorescent Ca²⁺ indicator) is read as fluorescence; aequorin is a luminescent/bioluminescent reporter.

Recognition, signalling and protection are separate experimental milestones. Cell death alone does not establish authentic or useful immunity.

Is killing your own cell really the immune response?

Yes — and the reason is that plants have none of the machinery the question assumes. There are no mobile immune cells, no antibodies and nothing that engulfs or digests a pathogen. No part of a plant travels to the site of infection. Every cell has to defend itself with what it already contains, so “get rid of the bacteria” is not an option the plant has.

What the intuition expects

Detect the invader, send cells to it, destroy and clear it, remove the toxins it released. That is roughly how vertebrate immunity works.

None of it is available here. In particular, there is no way to remove an effector once delivered. The plant cannot un-inject it or neutralise it.

What the plant actually does

It removes the resource instead of the pathogen. The infected cell and its immediate neighbours die deliberately and fast — the hypersensitive response (HR).

Most of these pathogens are biotrophs or hemibiotrophs: they need living host cells to feed from. Killing the cell destroys the niche, so the pathogen is starved and walled into dead tissue. It is containment by denial, not destruction.

HR is also only the most visible output, not the most important one. Alongside it the cell induces defence genes, makes antimicrobial compounds, reinforces its wall with callose, and raises salicylic acid, which primes the rest of the plant against later attack. A good receptor produces that programme. A receptor that only produces death has done the dramatic part and skipped the useful part.

So why is cell death not accepted as proof? Three reasons, and each has sunk real projects:
  • It is not specific. Plant cells die from overexpression, mis-folded protein, toxicity and ordinary stress. Seeing death tells you something happened, not that immune signalling happened.
  • It helps the wrong pathogens. Necrotrophs feed on dead tissue. Against them an HR is actively counterproductive.
  • Constant death is a dead plant. An autoactive receptor kills tissue with no pathogen present. That is a stunted or dead crop, which is why the OFF state is weighted so heavily throughout this reference.

This is the reasoning behind the “binding ≠ activation ≠ resistance” principle in section 01, and behind measuring calcium and defence-gene induction rather than photographing dead leaf patches. The target is a receptor that stays silent, then produces a genuine defence programme — with localised death as a possible consequence of that programme, not as the goal.

Where the effector names actually come from

The honest answer first: you do not discover effector names by searching a database. There is no “effector” field in UniProt, and most real effectors are deposited as “uncharacterised protein”. Names come from the literature; databases give you the sequence and structure once you already have a name. Work in that order and the problem stops being circular.

03. Reference receptor architecture

Start with evidence, then explore de novo designs

Pikm-1 sensor + Pik-2 helper

Schematic, not a scale model or established mechanical explanation of activation. CC, coiled-coil; HMA, heavy metal–associated integrated domain; NB-ARC, nucleotide-binding domain; LRR, leucine-rich repeats.

Established reference

The Pikm-1 integrated HMA site accommodates other HMA domains, nanobodies and AI-designed binders. Pik-2 partner identity affects function. This is a useful experimental baseline, not proof that any inserted binder will signal.

The advertised PhD's next challenge

Engineer new-to-nature receptor architectures with tractable expression, purification and structural characterization. This extends beyond swapping binders into an existing NLR and requires empirical ON/OFF-state characterization.

Sensor, helper, binder — three different things

An analogy that holds: the binder is the sensor head, the part you unscrew and replace. The sensor NLR is the alarm unit the head plugs into. The helper NLR is the siren wired to the alarm. Swapping the head changes what sets the alarm off; it does not change the alarm or the siren — which is exactly why the pairing has to be checked separately.

Why “compatibility” is a real variable and not bookkeeping. Sensor and helper have to be a matched pair. A binder that works perfectly can still give you an unusable receptor because the sensor it sits in and the helper it signals through are mismatched. The published case in section 05 is exactly this: Pikm-1OsHIPP43 was autoactive with Pikm-2 and became well behaved with Pikp-2 — same binder, same sensor, different helper, opposite outcome.

Is HMA the original bait, and are we replacing it?

Yes, and that is the whole reason this scaffold was chosen. Most NLRs detect effectors indirectly. A minority carry an integrated domain: a copy of a genuine host protein that has been fused into the receptor, so the receptor carries its own bait. In Pikm-1 that integrated domain is the HMA, derived from host heavy-metal-associated proteins that AVR-Pik effectors evolved to bind in the first place. The plant took the thing the pathogen attacks and wired it into an alarm.

So the engineering move is natural: remove the HMA and put a designed binder in its place, keeping the rest of the receptor. The position is already proven to accept a foreign recognition module — other HMA domains, nanobodies and AI-designed binders have all been inserted there.

One word to be careful with: “universal”. The designed binder targets a surface conserved across a structural family, so it is broader than one effector — but it is family-level, not universal. Calling it universal in an application or a talk will invite an immediate challenge about which effectors it does not cover. “Family-level” or “broad-spectrum” is both more accurate and more defensible.

Are we using Pikm-1, or is it just an example?

Both, and keeping the two roles separate matters:

Pikm-1 as platform

The validated chassis for building and testing. It gives you a working positive control, published structures, a known-compatible helper and existing plasmids. Early candidates go in here because it is the fastest route to an interpretable result.

It is also the benchmark any new architecture has to beat.

Pikm-1 as the thing to move past

The advert is explicit that the project extends beyond swapping binders into an existing NLR. Natural NLRs are biochemically awkward: hard to express, hard to purify, resistant to structural work for decades.

Succeeding only at a Pik graft would reproduce published work rather than extend it.

What “new-to-nature architecture” means — and what it does not

It does not mean taking Pikm-1 and bolting a fluorescent protein onto it. A fluorescent tag is an instrument; the architecture is the receptor itself. New-to-nature means the receptor polypeptide is a designed sequence that does not exist in any organism, built to keep the functional organisation of an immune receptor — a recognition module, a nucleotide-binding switch, a regulatory region — while being engineered from the start to behave in the laboratory: soluble, expressible, purifiable and tractable for crystallography or cryo-EM.

05. Engineering distinct OFF and ON states

The central unsolved mechanism

Engineering hypothesis: a recognition module can be coupled to a regulatory interface so effector binding favours the active receptor state, whereas the unbound receptor favours inactivity. The precise relay mechanism for engineered Pik receptors is not sufficiently known to design this switch with confidence from structure prediction alone.

Useful interpretation rules

  • pLDDT reports predicted local model confidence. It is not the probability of correct folding in the cell.
  • PAE / inter-chain confidence can flag uncertain domain positions or binding poses; it is not a quantitative affinity measurement.
  • RMSD can assess structural agreement after appropriate alignment. A low binder–target RMSD does not establish receptor signalling.
  • Energetic ON/OFF discrimination cannot be read directly from two independently predicted static structures.

07. Wet-lab and structural validation

Where crystallization belongs
1 · Build and verify constructs. Selected binder genes, receptor integrations, appropriate partner and controls.
2 · Validate protein production. Check receptor accumulation in plants; express and purify soluble binding partners separately where needed.
3 · Establish target association. Co-IP in the plant context and suitable biophysical assays (BLI, SPR or ITC) for purified binding partners.
4 · Assess plant ON/OFF switching. Run target-present / absent / unrelated tests before committing extensive structural resources.
5 · Crystallize promising binder–effector complexes. Determine atomic interface if crystallization and diffraction are successful; inspect in ChimeraX against prediction.
6 · Resolve receptor-state mechanism. Where tractable, use cryo-EM or X-ray for assembled complexes in distinct functional states, complemented by biochemical measurements.
7 · Test protection. Assess naturally occurring effector variants, actual infection and eventual stable lines if warranted.

Crystallography: what it answers

Whether a binder contacts the predicted surface, which residues interact and whether side-chain geometry matches the model. It may reveal unexpected binding orientations. It is often applied to a purified binder–target complex, not necessarily the entire NLR.

Cryo-EM: what it answers

For sufficiently stable complexes, it can reveal oligomeric architecture and large conformational rearrangements in inactive versus active receptor states. Capturing both states is difficult and may itself be a major PhD objective.

Verified structural precedent: the 2024 Pwl2–OsHIPP43 complex was determined by X-ray crystallography (PDB 8R7A), illuminating a robust effector-binding interface. It did not by itself solve the complete Pik receptor's ON/OFF mechanism.

Co-IP: how it works and how to read the blot

Co-immunoprecipitation asks one question: if I grab protein A out of a plant extract, does protein B come with it? The two proteins carry different tags so they can be grabbed and detected independently.

1 · Co-expressBait (binder or receptor, GFP-tagged) and prey (effector, HA-tagged) together in N. benthamiana leaf.
2 · ExtractGrind tissue in lysis buffer. Keep a small portion as the input — proof both proteins were made.
3 · CaptureAdd beads carrying anti-GFP antibody. They hold the bait, and anything bound to it.
4 · WashRinse away everything not held. Wash strength is the main dial between losing real partners and keeping junk.
5 · Elute & blotRun SDS-PAGE, transfer, probe once with anti-HA and once with anti-GFP.

What a clean positive result looks like

Each tag is detected separately. The anti-GFP row confirms the bait was captured; the anti-HA row reports whether the effector travelled with it.

No anti-GFP band in the IPThe capture failed — the bait was not pulled down.Check tag integrity and antibody. The anti-HA row is meaningless without this.
Bands present but input levels differ wildlyUnequal expression, so lane-to-lane comparison is unsafe.Normalise loading and repeat before claiming a difference in binding.

From a purified complex to a structure

The choice is driven by the sample, not by preference. A small rigid binder–effector complex is a crystallography problem; a large multi-subunit receptor that exists in more than one state is a cryo-EM problem. Section 11's reference interface (PDB 8R7A) came from the first route.

Making sure the binder grips the effector and nothing else

The binder will sit in a cytosol containing many thousands of other proteins, and it was designed against only one of them. Specificity is not a formality here — and it fails in a way that is easy to misdiagnose.

The failure mode to recognise. If the binder engages an endogenous plant protein, it may not look like a binding artefact at all. Inside a receptor, an off-target binding event can trigger the receptor — so the symptom is autoactivity: a receptor that fires with no effector present. This means the OFF-state test in section 05 is partly a specificity test, and an autoactive candidate should always raise “what else is it binding?” alongside “is the switch destabilised?”

09. Diagnose failures without wasting designs

Phenotype → likely causes → next evidence
ObservationPossible explanationFirst evidence to gather
Predicted binder does not bindWrong interface, target surface inaccessible, poor folding, target conformation differs.Check protein quality and target construct, inspect predicted vs experimental structure, compare binding controls.
Binder binds; receptor gives no responseSignal relay uncoupled, unsuitable junction/orientation, incompatible helper, bad localization or poor accumulation.Confirm expression and in-plant association, examine established helper pairings and structural context.
Receptor activates without targetDisrupted inhibitory interactions, altered integrated domain or sensor–helper incompatibility.Compare no-target controls, receptor abundance and published compatible helpers; examine junction variants.
Effector reduces spontaneous activationBinding may stabilize an inhibitory state rather than favour ON.Compare bound vs unbound biochemical/structural evidence and calcium/cell-death responses.
Activation with unrelated proteinsNonspecific binding, broadly stress-triggered response or intrinsically autoactive receptor.Cross-reactivity panel, protein accumulation and negative controls.
Cell death but no convincing calcium/gene signatureNonspecific toxicity, noncanonical signalling or assay timing mismatch.Time-resolved calcium, defence markers and reference receptor comparisons.
Works in transient assay; fails against diseaseTarget absent or inaccessible during natural infection, insufficient receptor expression, pathogen escape or downstream host constraints.Pathosystem-specific effector expression, isolate panel, infection phenotypes and host context.
Strong affinity but weak immunityInterface affinity and mechanical signal transmission are different variables.Compare binders with distinct orientation/geometry; avoid selecting by Kd alone.
Do not invent a universal “good” Kd, linker length, calcium peak or pLDDT cutoff. Thresholds depend on scaffold, target, assay, expression and positive-control behaviour. Define empirical progression criteria before screening each construct set.

11. Primary papers and exactly what they teach

Verify claims against the originals

Xi et al. (2026), bioRxiv preprint ↗

Directly relevant to Bentham's PhD. RFdiffusion + ProteinMPNN designed integrated domains for Pikm-1 against FoSSP17 from Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense TR4. Binding and structural accuracy did not uniformly predict signal relay. Preprint: subject to revision.

Zdrzałek et al. (2024), engineered OsHIPP43 receptor ↗

Natural effector-binding HMA domain replaces Pikm-1 HMA; Pikm-2 pairing autoactivated, whereas Pikp-2 allowed Pwl2-dependent response. Solved binder–effector crystal structure (PDB 8R7A). Practical precedent for binding, helper choice and structural validation.

Bentham et al. (2023), allelic compatibility ↗

Pik helper alleles regulate autoactivity; residue 230 is influential in tested combinations. Of four autoactive nanobody integrations, three were alleviated by Pikp-2, but one remained autoactive and another showed weaker signalling. Do not generalize helper rescue.

Kourelis et al. (2023), Pikobodies ↗

Nanobody modules can replace natural integrated recognition domains to produce ligand-dependent synthetic plant NLRs. Engineered constructs detected fluorescent targets and protected plants from engineered viruses.

Zhu et al. (2026), programmable SPIRs ↗

Programmable designed modules targeted viral, bacterial, fungal and oomycete proteins. In-planta directed evolution improved activity and reduced autoactivation; transgenic plants showed resistance. Demonstrates design-and-selection, not guaranteed first-pass success.

Durham PhD advert (2026) ↗

Two research strands: map structurally related effectors including SUSS families; design new-to-nature, tractable receptor architectures with RFdiffusion/ProteinMPNN/BindCraft, X-ray/cryo-EM and authentic calcium/gene-expression validation.

Additional software documentation: ChimeraX · RFdiffusion · ProteinMPNN · BindCraft. Software availability, licence and environment requirements may change.
